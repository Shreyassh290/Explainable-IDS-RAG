A distributed denial of service attack uses many systems at once to degrade or block the availability of a target. The traffic can be generated by multiple systems spread across the Internet, and botnets are commonly used for this purpose. Large botnets can produce a very large amount of traffic, and in the worst cases so many systems take part that each one needs to send only a small amount of traffic to saturate the target network. For example, an adversary can direct ten gigabits per second of traffic at a server whose network connection offers only one gigabit per second. Because the individual sources behave modestly, distinguishing the attack traffic from legitimate clients becomes very difficult. MITRE ATT&CK describes this as a network denial of service (T1498), and its direct network flood (T1498.001) sub-technique covers high-volume packet floods in which almost any protocol can be used, with UDP and ICMP common and TCP possible. Attackers may spoof source addresses, which hides the real systems and can be used to enable reflection.

At the flow level, a single flow from a distributed attack can look unremarkable: packet counts may be small and the packet rate moderate. The evidence is the pattern across many flows towards the same destination, with similar sizes and timing, and a sudden rise in volume. An analyst should therefore read the indicators of one flow together with the destination and the surrounding traffic.

NIST SP 800-61 gives an example of this kind of incident, in which an attacker commands a botnet to send high volumes of connection requests to a web server and causes it to crash. NIST SP 800-94 explains that network behavior analysis examines traffic to identify threats that generate unusual traffic flows, such as distributed denial of service attacks, and that it works from baselines of expected flows.

Mitigation starts with filtering. When the flood exceeds the capacity of the target connection, the traffic must be intercepted upstream by the Internet service provider, a content delivery network, or a provider that specializes in denial of service mitigation. Where the volume allows it, on-premises filtering can block the source addresses that send the attack, block the targeted ports, or block the protocols used for transport. Organizations should analyze the risk to critical resources, create a disaster recovery and business continuity plan, and engage third parties early, because the response may require them. Afterwards, the incident should be documented, indicators shared with partner organizations, and a lessons learned meeting held.
